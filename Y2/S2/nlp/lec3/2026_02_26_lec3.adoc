== What is morphology?

*Morphology* is the study of the internal structure of words + rules that form words from smaller meaningful units

* parts of complex words
** like medical terms
* lemmas, stems, etc

Bridges gap between phonology and syntax

== Morphemes

A morphene is the smallest meaningful unit in a language

* not necessarily a word
** indivisible unit

There are a few types:

. Free morphemes
* can be words
** blood
** pain
** heart

. Bound morphemes
* must be bound to other morphemes -> *affixes*
** prefixes, suffixes
** -itis
** -osis
** -ly
** un-

. Allomorphs
* variations of a morpheme determined by context
** "a" vs "an"

=== Morphemes in medical context

Medical language is a big hodgepodge of various languages

* <local language> + ancient greek + latin/pseudolatin + numbers + neologisms by doctors who think they're Shakespeare
** it's a whole mess

Standard NLP tools often fail on medical text

* reasons listed earlier
* greek/latin roots mean that words follow old morphological rules
** modern rules != old rules
** modern tools -> modern rules
* medical terms are often simply combinations of other terms
** meaning is the sum of the sub parts and nothing else

For example:

* brady (slow) + cardia (heart/of the heart/related to the heart) = bradycardia
** slow heart
* Gast (stomach) + r (for some reason) + itis (inflammation) = Gastritis
** stomach inflammation

With a few 100 affixes NLP systems can parse many more new terms

== Stemming

Stemming is a heuristic process that chops off the ends of words to hopefully arrive at the root

* quickly -> quick
** typically a bit more sophisticated than this

Stemming algorithms exist for many western languages

* not all languages are (easily) stemmable
** declension/conjugations/agglutinatives are very challenging

=== Porter Stemmer

Most common rule-based stemming algorithm

* English only (because of course)

5 cascaded steps

Fast but not very accurate

* over/under stemming is very common

=== Finite State Transducers (FST)

Morphology can be modeled formally using FSTs

* maps 2 strings
** upper/lexical
** lower/surface

==== FST Definition

FSTs are:

* compositional
** rules are expressed as chains of transducers
* bidirectional
** can be used to generate and analyze words

Formally an FST is a 6-tuple stem:[Q, \Sigma, \Gamma, I, F, \delta] such that:

* stem:[Q] is a finite set of states
* stem:[I] is a subset of stem:[Q]
** contains the set of initial states
* stem:[F] is a subset of stem:[Q]
** contains the set of final states
* stem:[\Sigma] is a finite set containing the input alphabet
* stem:[\Gamma] is a finite set containing the output alphabet
* stem:[\delta] is the *transition relation*
** Written as: stem:[\delta \subseteq Q \cdot (\Sigma \cup \{ \epsilon \}) \cdot (\gamma \cup \{\epsilon\}) \cdot Q]
*** where stem:[\epsilon] is the empty string
*** stem:[\delta] is a set of 6 tuples that each represent a possible transition between states based on input and ouput symbols
*** basically maps transitions between states

==== FSTs for morphology

FSTs map 2 strings

* literal string (i.e. the input tape of the FST)
* onto the same string annotated with morpheme markers

This process may go through intermediate representations

For example:

. Input string: FOXES
* raw string
. Intermediate representation: `FOX^s#`
* represents root word with plural marker
. Final mapping: `FOX+N+PL`
* represents root word again with plural suffix and word type marker

=== Stemming in the medical domain

Symbolic approaches are mostly fine

* predictable
* interpretable
* manual egineering so no training and black box models

With a few caveats:

* Hard to maintain (because of manual engineering)
* Brittle
* Language specific

== Lemmatization

More sophisticated than stemming

Reduces inflected or derived words to their dictionary form

* i.e. actually gets the root word
** better -> good
** running, runs, ran -> run

Uses multiple techniques/tools:

* vocabulary
* PoS context
* morphological analysis

Lemmatization is:

* Context aware
** uses surrounding words to determine intended meaning
** distinguishes between parts of speech
* Accurate (I guess??)
** produces actual valid dictionary words
** no truncated nonsense

=== Statistical Lemmatization

PoS tagging is required

* disambiguates meaning
* allows for accurate retrieval

Can use a probabilistic approach

* find lemma stem:[L] given a word stem:[W] and its PoS tag stem:[T]
** stem:[\hat{L} = \text{argmax}_L P(L | W, T)]

Probabilities are estimated based on *corpus*

* collection of text
** consisting of `(word, lemma)` pairs

Using techniques like:

* HMMs
* Conditional Random Fields

This can also be used to predict lemma and PoS given a word + context

* not really used in medical domain

=== Neural Lemmatization

Lemmatization can be viewed as a seq2seq task on tokens

* encoder reads word
* decoder returns lemma+marker

== Morphology in the modern age

LLMs ignore morphology

* LLMs are entirely token based

This is bad for medical domain

* morphology carries lots of meaning
** omitting it equals losing a lot of meaning
** which is bad

=== Subword tokenization

Text is tokenized by *frequent subword units*

* bits of words

This captures morphological units implicitly

HOWEVER:

* the captured units may not be valid morphemes/may be ambiguous
** `"unhappiness"` -> `["un", "happi", "ness"]`
*** "un" can be "un" (without) or "un" as in "un-ity"

=== Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)

BPE is a relatively simple text encoding algorithm

. find most frequent pair `ab`
. replace `ab` with new value `c`
. write mapping somewhere that `{"c": "ab"}`
. repeat until sufficiently compressed

In medical text this naturally finds common medical units because they appear together very frequently:

* itis
* ology
* cardi

This works but can be noisy

* can find meaningless units
* can find false matches
