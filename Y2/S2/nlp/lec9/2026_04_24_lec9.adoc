== Distributional Semantics

Originated from linguistics

* "The meaning of a word is defined by the company it keeps"
** language is descriptive

Z.S. Harris says:

* if 2 words A and B are found interchangeably in the same environment they are synonyms
** against usual prescriptive dogma that says 2 words are synonyms if the dictionary says so

J.R. Firth says:

* The meaning of a word A *is* its environment
** not necessarily what the dictionary says

=== The Distributional Hypothesis

The meaning of a word is not a single entity but rather a set of relations

This goes against the Symbolic/Innate View (Chomsky)

* language is innate through Universal Grammar
** specific languages are versions of UG
* syntax precedes meaning
** syntactly correct but meaningless sentences are perfectly valid

Meaning is acquired through exposure to data and not internal biology

* language is learned by example
** language is pattern matching
*** especially co-occurrences
*** basis of statistics/ML
* meaning comes before syntax
** semantic value is irrespective of syntax

=== Emergent Semantics

Semantics is entirely emergent from sufficiently complex systems

* comes from statistical distribution of symbols
** computed from context
** not stored in KBs or books

=== Formalizing Context

With V = vocab, D = doc collection

The context of a word w in V is a multiset of words occurring in D in a window of size k around w

* if 2 words are used in the same context they likely share semantic properties
** proportion, not count/calculation

=== Term-Document Matrix

Matrix M -> V * D, M_ij = count of word i in doc j

* nice and simple
* however:
** big docs have more words -> numerically dominate small docs
** common words appear much more often -> numerically dominate rarer words

This is where TF-IDF can come in handy

=== Term-Context Matrix

CAUTION: by word we mean instance of a word

We drop the idea of a document and we focus on context

* theoretically single documents don't influence meaning

V^2 matrix

* each term is a column
* each term is also a row

We add 1 for every word in the matrix that appears in the context of the current target word

This gives us 3 statistics:

. row sum for target
. column sum for context
* how many target words am i in context with?
. total windowed pairs

This will produce skewed data

* fine for now
* can deal with it later

CAUTION: these matrices tend to be *very* sparse. More sparse if the context size is too big

==== Better Context Definition

Setting a fixed window of -k +k is kind of sad

* more of a window than real semantic context

Many types of defining context:

* Dependency features
** size k neighborhood on graph built using dependency tree
** preserves relationship between relatively distant words that are related by dependency parsin
* Positional Context
** takes distance into account
** context c is a pair -> (c, n) where c is the word, n is the number of hops
* Directional Context
** sentences are sequential -> reading the same sentence backwards may or may not make sense

==== Pointwise Mutual Information

We can normalize the statistics from earlier with the total counts to get probabilities

We can then use these to define a PMI

* we can also force positive values for rare cases using `max`
** if we get a negative log -> set to 0 -> no big deal

=== Count-Wise Embeddings

We can use PPMI to generate count based embeddings

* LexVec
* GloVe
* WOVe

Since the TCM is sparse the PPMI matrix will also be sparse -> can be compressed using:

* Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
** can't reconstruct matrix completely
** can approximate a new matrix with highly similar values
* Non Negative Matrix Factorization
* QR with pivot

Pick one and go with it

* SVD tends to produce the best approximation

Once they are defined they can be saved and they never change (provided we use the same corpora)

* they are *static*

Even with SVD, PPMI are very sparse and very high dimensional

* storage takes O(n^2)
* SVD computation takes O(n^3) time (!!!) and O(n^2) space (!!!)

What if we could predict a decent approximation of a denser embedding matrix?

== Neural Embeddings

We could a model to predict a word given its context

If prediction is good:

* model weights must necessarily be able to encode the meaning

=== Word2Vec

Combines CBOW and skip-grams using log-linear models

* having the same word 3 times doesn't increase the meaning by 3
** have to find some other way of representing words linearly

*CBOW* tries to predict the center word

* Takes in one-hot encoded vectors of the k-context words
* input vecotrs are multiplied by weight matrix and averaged/summed
** they lose their order

*Skip-gram* tries to predict the context of the center word

* takes in one hot encoded vector of central word w_t
* produces

==== Context Window Size

Low k values favor close distance sytactic and functional similarity

* more mechanical
* syntactic role

High k values favor topical and associative similarity

* semantic role

In DH -> medium/high K tend to be better

* medical text doesn't always follow the classical syntax rules

==== Analogy and Arithmetic

Word2Vec spaces have linear compositionality

* which is a big deal
* it encodes *linguistic analogy*

Captures syntactic and semantic relations

==== Issues with Word2Vec

Very influential but has some issues

. Entirely local
* based on k previous and next words
* misses global context of document
. Order independent
* based on bag of words that ignores order
** order of words can change semantics
. Contex independent
* each word has 1 associated vector
** polysemous words are averaged (bad)
. Blind to OOV words
* word not in training -> word doesn't exist
. Skewed towards common words
* rare words get bad vectors

=== GloVe

Uses a different context definition

* tries to get *global* context
* uses co-occurrence matrix of all members of the document
** matrix of size `n_words * n_words` where cells hold number of co-occurrences
** uses a bias and a weighting function to avoid common pairs dominating the loss

It minimizes the difference between the dot product of 2 vectors and the log of their co-occurrence frequency

* basically quantifies how anomalous a co-occurrence pair is
* can learn how words relate to each other relative to other words
** penumonia is related to cough
** hepatitis is related to cough differently

=== Saving Morphology

Word2Vec and GloVe operate on *single words*

* 1 word = 1 vector
* we lose morphology

Need to preserve morphology somehow

=== FastText

Developed by Facebook (pre-Meta) for sentence classification

* really fast
* low memory
* handles typos and stuff

Represents words as a bag of n-grams

* typically `n in range(3, 6)` for English but can be learned
** e.g. `Hepatitis = [Hepa, epat, pati, atit, titi, itis]`

[CAUTION]
.Picking n-grams
====
Choosing the right `n` can be tricky

* Medical language can require high `n` (5, 6, 7) that can filter out smaller relevant morphemes
* Too small -> not useful,
* Too large -> can't isolate
====

Vectors are leared for each n-gram and scored with context vector

* context windows
* context paths
* whatever
* certain n-grams should be more informative than others

==== FastText for DH

FT is good for DH:

. Handles morphology
* can learn suffixes regardless of root word
** e.g. -itis => inflammation
. Robust to typos
* very similar words will have similar n-grams/high n-gram overlap
. Handles OOV terms
* can construct vectors from n-grams
** knows what n-grams mean
. Very efficient
* smaller vocabs using n-grams

=== The problem with Static Embeddings

Regardless of exact method we still get 1 word = 1 vector independent from context

* In NL context can change the meaning of a word
* Static embeddings sort of average all the known meanings of a word
** opposing meanings will average to 0

=== Contextual embeddings

Instead of mapping 1 word to 1 vector we can map an *instance* of a word to a vector

* The same word in a a different context will have similar but different vectors

Models can generate a vector for a word given a context

* using a larger context

==== Using Sequential Models

We could use a sequential model (RNN, LSTM) since NL can be interpreted as a sequence of tokens

However:

. Parallelizability
* sequential models can't easily be parallelized
* really bad for efficiency
** bottlenecking happens

. Forgetfulness
* early tokens in long sequences can lose their power
** *vanishing gradient*

So what do we do?

== Transformers

They are probably our best bet

* easy to parallelize
* very large context windows
** constant path length -> all pairs are considered in context
** regardless of word distance

=== Encoder-Decoder Architectures

Made of 2 parts:

. Encoders generate a deep bidirectional representation of the input sequence
* highly semantic representation
. Decoders use autoregressive generation with masked attention
* generate output from semantic representation given by encoder

These rely on:

. multi-head attention
. position-wise FFNNs

=== Positional Encoding

We want to work on sets and keep order/positional information

This changes for even or odd position

* even: stem:[\sin \frac{\text{pos}}{10,000^{2i/d_{model}}}]
* odd:  stem:[\cos \frac{\text{pos}}{10,000^{2i/d_{model}}}]

10,000 is arbitrary

* just needs to be larger than the size of the vector

==== Why sinusoidal PE?

. Linear relationships
* can use affine matrices to rotate the encoding (???)
* self attention can learn relationships by using their relative position
. Translation invariance
* the dot product of the encodings is inversely correlated with size of k
** learns locality bias

At the end of the day it's an additive position embedding method

==== Other options

The basic idea is still to encode position as a geometric transformation

. Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)
* used by modern LLMs
* multiplicative
* like 2% more efficient

=== Attention Mechanism

Attention is used to weigh parts of the context

* more important -> high weight

==== Content-Addressable memory

Accessing by index is kind of useless

We can treat a sentence as a *searchable database*

* each word asks something to other words

Using *Query*, *Key*, *Value* to do role-based

* soft lookup
* answers are fuzzy

.Query
* Matrix *Q*
* sort of a question
** token asks rest of sentence
** "I am a dosage. Which drug am I referring to?"

.Key
* Matrix *K*
** words are labeled according to what they offer
** "I am Warfarin. My label says Drug"

.Value
* Matrix *V*
** payload conveying semantics
** "this is the semantic vector for Warfarin"

==== Multiple Perspectives

The same word can convey different meanings to other words

* doctor POV -> girl is patient
* Warfarin POV -> girl is consumer

Different QKV projections separate roles

* Word's Q can look for Patient role
* Word's K can signal its role
** consumer, subject, patient
* Words's V encodes a different relation for each attention head

==== Relevance

How do we weigh the word/token?

The same input token X is projected into 3 different spaces -> model can filter by relevance

stem:[Q \cdot K] computes how well a pair of terms match -> use match score to get weighted V sum

* Q, K, V are learned during training
* projects embeddings into questions, keys, values

Sentences become *dynamic graphs*

* no longer sequence of vectors

==== Attention

Attention is:

[stem]
++++
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}V
++++

In Multi-Head Attention we use multiple attention heads to ask more questions to find the best

* asking a single question is not that informative
* we can easily ask multiple questions in parallel

Concatenate compressed output of multiple attention heads

* for each head we get independent projection matrices

Questions become more and more abstract as we pass through more and more transformer blocks

* T1 = who did what?
* T2 = <something abstract>

=== Position-wise Feed-Forward blocks

Usually 2 layer MLP with ReLU between them

* higher dimensionality than transformer
** like 4x transformer dimension

Parameters are free

* totally unconstrained
* transformer can kind of do whatever

Model can learn semantic indicators

=== Encoder vs Decoder

Bidirectional encoders allow tokens to attend to all other tokens

* good for understanding tasks and information extraction

Causal masking in decoders

* prevents information flow from future tokens
** can't use the future to generate the future

== Advanced Architectures

=== BERT (2018)

Instead of predicting k from seq[:k] tokens it looks at the entire sequence forward and backward

* uses only encoder part of transformer
* trained on tons of unlabelled text data
** fine-tuned for specific tasks

Training loop:

. Randomly take a pair of maksed sentences
* enables lots of applications for free
** classification, IR/IE, question answering, dialogue, semantic relationships, etc.
** apparently old idea from ancient logicians
** pairs can be used to model all of mathematics
. Tokenize
. Embed (normal and special tokens)
. Feed to transformers and such

Then fine-tuned (supervised)

* task specific
* weights are modified according to specific training

==== Tokenization

Uses WordPiece

* similar to BPE
* joins 2 tokens if they appear together more often than their individual probabilities

Produces k vectors per word unlike FastText and BPE

==== Next Sentence Prediction

Can also be used for *Next Sentence Prediction*

* frame training as binary classification
** given a pair SA, SB, can SB follow SA in the original text?

==== Masked Language Model

Most famous for *Masked Language Model*

Uses 3 special tokens:

. CLS
* marks starting of pair
. SEP
* marks separation
. MASK
* marks hidden bit of text

How?

. take sequence
. randomly hide 15% of tokens
* of this 15%:
** 80% of the time hide with MASK
** 10% of the time replace with random word
** 10% of the time leave original word
** prevents chicanery if encounters OOV at inference time

Learns to fill in the blanks by predicting hidden tokens
