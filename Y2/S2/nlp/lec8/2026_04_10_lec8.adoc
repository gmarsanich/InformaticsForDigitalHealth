== What is semantics?

Semantics is the study of meaning in language and logic

* we operate on meanings when we intake a bit of language and have to parse it...
* ...and reason on it -> apply logical operations and produce more language to express output

.Symbolic semantics
Represents knowledge by discrete (enumerable) symbols

* UMLS is a set of enumerable symbols
** it's basically an alphabet
* meaning is defined by explicit logical relationships
** each symbol has one or more meanings
** explaining the meaning may require more symbols/language but not necessarily
* explainable, verifiable, precise, human readable

.Connectionist semantics
Represents knowledge as a point in a *continuous* high dimensional vector space

* extremely robust
** smaller error propagation with smaller differences
* handles nuance
** similar but distinct symbols will be increasingly far from each other
* scales with data
* largely uninterpretable

=== Types of Symbolic Semantics

3 main types:

. Lexical semantics
* given a symbol -> get meaning
* get relation between symbols from meaning
* works on *base case*
** semantics of single words

. Compositional semantics
* composing meanings of single words
** i.e. builds complete semantics of a sentence from single constituent parts
* works *recursively*
** knowing the semantics of these words -> build overall semantics

. Contextual semantics
* looks outside the span of text
** "where does this span come from?"
* the origin of a span of text can change its semantics
* lives in the *global environment*
** not part of the sentence/bit of language

== Lexical Semantics

* even more structured than normal dictionaries
* makes pointer structure of dictionaries real

Groups words by *meaning* and establishes *relationships* between these groups

. Constitutive

** antonymy: opposite
*** gradable, simple, converse
*** not necessarily polar opposites
** synonymy: same meaning
** etc.

. Role
* Agent
** indicates doer of the thing
* Patient
* Result
** indicates the outcome of the thing
* Instrument
** indicates object used to do the thing
* Location
* Direction
* Target
* Source
* Co-role

. Derivation
* diminutive
* augmentative

. Participle

. Pertainyim

* is pertinent to

. Domain
* topic
** thing is relevant in this topic
* region
* exemplifies
** this is an example of this concept

. Other
* mostly for human readability


WordNet has 4 classes (basically the open word classes)

* nouns
* verbs
* adjectives
* adverbs

==== Synsets

A *synset/sense* is a unique cluster of words that can be interchangeably used to describe a particular meaning

A word can be in multiple synsets

* words can have multiple meanings

Synsets contain only one of the 4 classes

* some relationships cross the class boundary

Distinct synset of synonyms

== Case Grammars and Frame Semantics

Larger meanings

* bits of language
** not single terms

=== Filmore's case Grammars (1968)

Verbs are usually classified as:

* transitive
* intransitive

Works syntactically rather than semantically

Filmore proposed *verb cases/frames*

* verbs have properties and structures
** keys are fixed with specific types
** verbs can be filled with references to bits of the sentence

Initial idea worked syntactically but not semantically

* grammars defined with this idea were often unusable

Redefined as *semantic frames*

=== Frame Semantics

Frames are descriptions of a type of:

. event
. or a relation
. or an entity+its participants

Each frame contains:

* a doer
** thing eating
* a doee
** thing being eaten

Each frame consists of:

* lexical units that evoke the frame (LUs)
** basically a list of words that summon the frame
* frame elements (FEs)
** describe the *slots/roles* of the frame
** some slots are optional (or *peripheral*)

==== LUs and Frames

The word *lung* matches the LU *lung.n*

We can find out in which frames the LU appears in

* in this case it could be *body parts*

Which FEs constitute *body parts*?

* *owner* is needed (core)
** this lung obviously belongs to someone
* other supplemental info
** *body part* is also needed (core)
* *descriptor* is peripheral
* *subregion* is peripheral
* *orientational location* is peripheral
* *attachment* is peripheral

Which other LUs *can* serve as body parts?

* many
** LUs can appear in other frames

=== Frame Relations

There are various types of frame relations:

* Inheritance
** body part inherits *shaped part*
** body part is inherited by other frames
* Perspective
* Using
* Subframe
* Metaphor
** metaphors evoke frames linked with literal meaning and metaphorical meaning
* etc.

==== The Concept of Role

Roles are mostly disconnected from syntax

* a sentence with the same meaning can be rewritten with different (legal syntaxes)

The role of *agent* only applies to something with intention

Roles can be used to understand the meaning of a piece of language

* syntax isn't really necessary

We can *reason* on frames using this concept

* semantic roles can push reasoning in different directions
** can ignore/accept different parts (e.g. the subject)

=== Finding Frames in Text

3 step program:

. Find triggers
* Find LUs that evoke specific frames
. Classify evoked frames
* which frames do these triggers classify
. Extract the argument and fill the slots

There is a Python library that does this as a *seq2seq* task using transformers

* using ML/DL to do *symbolic* tasks
** symbolic tasks are explainable by default
** ML tasks are not
** we lose explainability

=== Limitations

The idea of Frame Semantics is fine

* the application is a human thing
** i.e. not very scalable

As languages evolve -> frames need to evolve

* need to keep up
* highly specialized domains are largely unsupported
** i.e. the medical domain is unsupported (booo)

The hypothesis was that AI fails because it lacks common sense

* it lacks the unspoken rules we use to navigate reality
** lots of knowledge cannot be easily expressed with language

So they wanted to encode these rules and somehow teach them to an AI
